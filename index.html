Principled trust in autonomy.

A system earns trust not by acting confidently, but by knowing when it should not.

PI: Eunjeong Hyeon University of California, Riverside

About

We SHIFT the paradigm for intelligent systems.

SHIFT Lab studies a fundamental problem in autonomous systems: how should a system make decisions when the information behind them is uncertain, incomplete, or unreliable?

A system earns trust not by acting confidently, but by knowing precisely when it should not.

We develop principled methods to quantify uncertainty and integrity, determine when predictions can be trusted, and translate that information into safer decisions and control.

Research

One question, three registers

Our research spans three registers of one question: what an autonomous system can trust in its own sensing and learned models (runtime integrity monitoring), how it should act on forecasts it cannot fully trust (decision-aware prediction and risk-aware control), and how it should reach agreements with other agents it cannot fully observe (negotiated multi-agent coordination). Methods are validated from simulation through hardware-in-the-loop to on-track tests on production vehicles, and have been applied to UAV navigation and connected and automated vehicles.

Runtime Integrity Monitoring for AI Systems

Learned perception and prediction models report confidence, not integrity. We build protection levels for AI outputs by extending the fault-hypothesis structure of GNSS receiver autonomous integrity monitoring to learned models, with conformal prediction calibrating the nominal case. The result is a runtime test that tells a controller when an AI output may be used and when it must not.

Decision-Aware Prediction and Risk-Aware Control

A forecast exists to serve a decision. We design predictors and controllers as a pair: the predictor is trained on the closed-loop cost of its errors rather than on average accuracy, and the controller hedges explicitly against the uncertainty the predictor reports. This thrust links the other two: integrity bounds become the uncertainty a controller consumes, and predictions of other agents feed coordination.

Negotiated Coordination Under Partial Information

Agents with different objectives and partial views must decide whether to cooperate, on what plan, over a channel that drops and delays messages. We study agreement-seeking cooperation: decision policies, message sets and timing, and models of how often agreement forms, validated from simulation to experiment.
